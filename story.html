My story

The slightly longer version — how I ended up caring about vision, medicine, and systems that ship.

I got into machine learning the way a lot of people do: I built something that sort of worked, and then couldn’t stop thinking about why it failed.

Early on, it wasn’t the “magic” of neural nets that hooked me — it was the gap between demos and reality. A model would look great on a curated test set, and then crumble when the lighting changed, the camera moved, or the input looked even a little different than expected. That mismatch felt less like an annoyance and more like a puzzle: what does it take to make learning systems behave reliably?

Stanford Medicine

In 2022, I started research with Stanford University School of Medicine in biomedical informatics. The setting was different from the typical “build it and ship it” energy: the problems were real, the constraints were real, and the cost of mistakes mattered.

Working on cancerous skin lesion detection taught me a lot about the parts of ML people skip in blog posts: dataset quirks, distribution shifts, label noise, and the importance of evaluation that matches the world you care about. I learned to treat failure cases as first-class data — not as embarrassment, but as guidance.

Where generative models fit in

Around the same time, I was drawn to generative models because they force you to think about structure. In my stereoscopic image work, I explored three approaches: a Pix2Pix-style conditional GAN, a depth-map-based analytical method, and a composite approach that blended both.

The fun part wasn’t just getting a score to go up — it was understanding why each method failed, and how their failure modes differed. The hybrid approach worked better because it didn’t bet everything on one idea. That pattern shows up everywhere: strong systems are often ensembles of complementary assumptions.

Building products keeps me honest

Research sharpened my taste for careful evaluation, but building products taught me something else: a model isn’t “done” when it hits a number. It’s done when the surrounding system—data pipeline, latency, monitoring, user feedback—makes the output dependable.

That’s a big reason I enjoy working as CTO at Appraise AI. The project is applied, but not trivial: you have image understanding, retrieval over messy real listings, and the task of turning all that into a single number someone can trust. I like that the system has to be clear about uncertainty, and I like that reality pushes back immediately when you cut corners.

What I’m aiming for

Going forward, I want to keep living at the intersection of research and deployment: methods that improve robustness and generalization, plus the engineering discipline to make them useful.

If you’re working on something adjacent—medical AI, vision, generative modeling, evaluation, or ML systems—feel free to reach out. I’m always happy to compare notes.
