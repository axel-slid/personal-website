My story

The longer version — why I care about robustness, physiology, and systems that actually work.

I got into machine learning the same way I get into most things: I tried to build something, it worked just enough to be exciting, and then it failed in a way I couldn’t ignore.

The failure wasn’t subtle. A model could look great on a tidy dataset and then fall apart the moment the real world got involved: different lighting, different sensors, different demographics, different hospitals. I didn’t want “cool demos.” I wanted to understand what it takes for a system to be reliable when you don’t control the conditions.

Finding the right constraints

In 2022 I started research with Stanford’s Center for Biomedical Informatics Research. Medical ML is a good place to learn humility: you can’t hand-wave data quality, and you can’t pretend distribution shift is an edge case. The stakes force you to be concrete.

My work has touched a few threads that all point in the same direction: reduce spurious correlations, build better augmentations, and evaluate in ways that match deployment. When a model fails, the interesting question is almost always “what assumption did we bake in without noticing?”

Mechanistic structure: when physics helps

One of my favorite directions has been using physiology and simulation to create more realistic training variation. For lung imaging, we simulated respiratory motion with finite element analysis to model breathing-cycle differences—then used those simulations as augmentations that improved segmentation performance.

I like this line of work because it’s not just “more data.” It’s structured variation: the model sees shifts that actually occur in the world, which is a different kind of generalization than random perturbations.

Building products keeps me honest

Research gives you taste. Shipping gives you discipline. As CTO & co-founder at Appraise AI, I spend time on the unglamorous parts: data ingestion, retrieval over messy marketplaces, latency, monitoring, and how to communicate uncertainty.

I like that real users force clarity. If something breaks, you don’t get to argue with a metric—you fix the system. That feedback loop is part of why I enjoy living at the intersection of research and deployment.

What I’m aiming for

Going forward, I want to keep working on methods that improve robustness and generalization—especially for vision—and keep grounding those methods in realistic evaluation and usable systems.

If you’re working on adjacent problems, feel free to reach out. The best collaborations I’ve had usually started with a simple message and a shared curiosity.
